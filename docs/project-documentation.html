01What AapatVani is

AapatVani is a disaster-information system for the hill districts of Nepal. It takes the fragments a community sends during a monsoon event — a Nepali text message, a photograph, a forwarded message, a voice note from someone who saw it from a bus window — and turns them into one structured incident record a responder can act on.

The chain is the product: community evidence → incident intelligence → potential impact analysis, with provenance at every link. Most tools in this space stop at the second step, or at the first. A pin on a map tells a district officer that something happened somewhere. It does not tell them that four villages have just lost every mapped road route to a hospital whose nearest alternative is thirty kilometres away.

The one-sentence version

During an event a district does not suffer from too few reports — it suffers from too many it cannot verify. AapatVani takes what the community already sends and turns it into one incident, with the copies and contradictions exposed and the road-access consequence computed.

What makes it different

Ordinary tools What AapatVani does instead
Crisis-mapping platforms collect reports and place pins, fast. Reasons over the reports: groups them, excludes copies, flags contradictions, then computes what the blockage means for road access to services.
Hazard and satellite models predict flood extent, runout, damage. Deliberately predicts no hazard. It answers a different, answerable question: what does what communities report imply for service access?
Government portals (Nepal's BIPAD and similar) are the official record. Built to work alongside that world: community evidence, provenance-labelled, which a reviewer has confirmed before it is worth anything.
LLM summarisation reads many reports and writes fluent prose. The summary is falsifiable. Every model output is schema-validated, citation-checked, and downgradeable when it cannot be supported.

Three properties enforced in code, not policy

  1. Provenance is mechanical. The excerpt check, the alias map, the coordinate rule and the downgrade path are all tested. A claim the model cannot support is not softened — it is relabelled, and the label is visible on screen.
  2. Copies are a first-class failure mode. Counting independent witnesses rather than reports is a small idea with a large effect on whether a responder trusts what they are reading. One forwarded message that looks like fifty confirmations is the most common way community-sourced information misleads.
  3. The system abstains. A report with no usable location is never guessed at. A place outside the dataset is reported as unchecked, never as safe.

02The problem, and why Nepal

Nepal's monsoon runs roughly June to September. Every year it triggers thousands of landslides across the middle hills. The characteristic failure is not the landslide itself — it is that a hillside road is the only road. A single slip at one point on the Arniko Highway can sever the connection between a dozen villages and their nearest health facility, sometimes for days.

The information problem sits on top of the physical one. Reports arrive through whatever channel is nearest to hand: a phone call to a relative, a Facebook post, a Viber message forwarded four times, a photo with no place attached. A district officer then has to work out, quickly and under pressure, which of these describe the same event, which are copies, which contradict each other, and what it all means for the villages downstream.

Why Nepal specifically

Condition Why it makes this the right place to build
Single-road dependency Hill settlements are connected by one mapped road. This makes the road-access question unusually decisive — the answer is often binary, not incremental.
High mobile penetration, low friction to report A web page works. Nobody needs to install an application for an event that happens twice a year, and no account is required.
Language and literacy Nepali is the working language of the affected districts, and typing Devanagari on a phone is a real barrier. Voice notes are not a nicety here; they are the difference between a person reporting and not.
Existing institutional context Nepal already has BIPAD, the national disaster portal. The gap is not an official record — it is community evidence that has been checked. Working alongside an existing system is a better problem than filling a vacuum.
Open map data quality OpenStreetMap coverage in Nepal is good enough to route over, and honest enough that its gaps can be reported as gaps rather than hidden.
Transferability Every mechanism here — the road graph, the gazetteer, the hazard list, the region label — is configuration, not code. The same system applies to any road-constrained, monsoon-affected region.

The demo area

The prototype covers the Araniko Highway corridor in Sindhupalchok district: Dolalghat–Chautara–Bahrabise–Tatopani–Kodari, inside Nepal's border. This is a real OpenStreetMap extract, not a toy graph, and it contains a genuine, verifiable consequence:

If this stretch of the Arniko Highway is blocked What the engine computes
Between Larcha and Tatopani Tatopani, Namtan and Kodari lose every mapped road route to a health facility. The nearest alternative is 23–30 km away.
Near Ghumthang 20 settlements' routes to care grow by 14–15 km.
Near Khukundol 4 settlements' routes grow by 12 km.

These numbers come from the shipped dataset and can be reproduced with npm run bench and the impact panel in the running app.

03What happens when someone reports

This is the whole pipeline, in order. Each stage names what it produces and who or what decides.

1 · Submit
Reporter
A phone browser posts a form to /api/reports. The server checks origin, rate limit and size, validates the fields, stores media to private storage, hashes each file, reads EXIF, and writes the report with a reference code. The report is saved before any model is called, so a Gemini outage can never lose a submission.
2 · Extraction
Gemini, then code
Gemini reads the text, photos and voice note and returns structured JSON. The server then checks it independently: schema validation, alias mapping, excerpt verification against the reporter's own words, and gazetteer resolution for place names. Coordinates are never accepted from the model.
3 · Matching
Code, then Gemini
Candidate pairs are filtered by region, time and distance, with compatible hazards. Text embeddings give a cross-lingual similarity score. Copies are detected by photo hash, near-identical wording, and reporter token. Gemini then gives a pairwise verdict citing claims — but a score never merges anything.
4 · Review
A person
In the reviewer workspace the original sits beside what was read from it, with excerpts highlighted on hover. Confirm, correct, reject or ask for more — per claim, and per grouping. Every action writes to an append-only audit trail.
5 · Impact
Code
When a claim describes a road blockage, the engine snaps it to a road segment, removes that segment from the real graph, and re-runs multi-source shortest paths from every health facility. Each settlement is classified lost, longer or unchanged. Runs in ~11 ms.
6 · Alert
Gemini, then a person
A draft is built from confirmed facts only. Every sentence cites its sources; sentences with no valid citation, or a number not present in their sources, are removed and counted. A named reviewer approves it. The app never broadcasts.

What comes out the other end

Not "forty reports". Instead: one landslide at Larcha. Three independent witnesses, one forwarded copy excluded. One report disagrees about whether the road is open. If that segment is blocked, four settlements lose every mapped route to a health facility — the nearest was 21 to 30 km away.

04The trust model

This is the part that makes the rest usable. Every claim, finding and map feature carries an origin and a review status, and the interface never lets either be invisible.

Origin What it means What is stored with it
Reported A community member said it. The report ID and the verbatim excerpt, verified against the original.
AI interpretation Gemini read it from text, a photo or audio. Model ID, prompt version, and the cited excerpt or media item.
Computed Deterministic code over our datasets. Dataset version, parameters and inputs.
Reviewer A person entered or corrected it. User ID, timestamp and note.

Review status runs unreviewed → confirmed · edited · rejected · needs_info. The word Confirmed appears in exactly one situation: after a named person has acted. Everything else is awaiting review.

The three rules the model is not trusted with

  1. Gemini never sees or returns real record IDs. It cites short aliases — R1 for the report, M1 for a photo, A1 for a voice note — which the server maps back. An alias it does not recognise causes the claim to be dropped.
  2. Every quoted excerpt must appear in the original text, character for character. If it does not, the claim is downgraded to "AI interpretation, excerpt not verified" and flagged, rather than quietly kept.
  3. Coordinates never come from the model. They come only from a GPS fix, a dropped pin, or a gazetteer match against real map data. A report with no usable location is deliberately never distance-compared, rather than guessed at.
Privacy. Photographs sent to the model have their EXIF block stripped first — the reporter agreed to a machine reading their picture of a blocked road, not to their coordinates going to a third party with it. The stored original is untouched, so a reviewer still sees the true file. No account, name or phone number is ever required of a reporter.

Design principles that shaped the build

  • Swappable backends. DATA_BACKEND=local|firestore, MEDIA_BACKEND, AUTH_MODE=passcode|firebase. The whole application runs on a laptop with no cloud credentials and moves to Google Cloud with environment variables only. This is why it could be built and tested for a week before the first credential existed.
  • Server-only data access. Rules deny all client access to Firestore and Storage. Every read is a session-checked route handler. Access control lives in one place.
  • Save first, analyse second. A report is stored and given a reference code before any model call.
  • Deterministic geography. The same inputs always give the same road analysis. That is tested, and it is what makes the impact figures reproducible.

06Running it

Locally, with no credentials at all

npm install
npm run seed        # the demo scenario: 6 synthetic reports over real road geography
npm run dev         # http://localhost:3000

Open http://localhost:3000 for the public report form. Reviewers sign in at /login with the passcode demo-reviewer (local development only).

No credentials are needed for any of this. Without a Gemini key the app still stores reports and marks them "awaiting a person". Review, grouping, contradiction detection and the road-impact analysis all work, because they are deterministic code rather than model calls.

07What each user experiences

A community reporter

They open a web page on a phone. There is no application to install, no account, and no name to give. The interface is in Nepali by default, with English one tap away.

  • They describe what happened — by typing, by taking a photo, or by speaking.
  • They pick a hazard from the region's own list, with a plain-language description beside each name, because "landslide" asks a frightened person to already know the right word.
  • They give a location three ways: a GPS fix, a pin dropped on a map, or simply the name of the place.
  • They say roughly when it happened.

They receive a reference code and a receipt page showing what the system understood — their own words, the places recognised, the time interpreted. That page is theirs alone, gated on a token their browser holds; it deliberately shows no reviewer notes, no grouping, and no incident data.

If their place name is not in our map data, the app says so plainly rather than showing nothing: "named by the reporter, not on our map", with the consequence stated — that report was not compared with others by distance.

A reviewer

They sign in and land on a dashboard answering four questions in the order a shift runs: how much is waiting, where things are, which incidents exist, and what arrived recently.

  • The review queue and report workspace: the original beside what was read from it. Hovering a claim highlights its excerpt in the Nepali text.
  • The incident workspace: membership, scored match suggestions with a visible feature breakdown, and contradictions — each with confirm / correct / reject per claim.
  • The impact panel: run the road analysis, see the assumed blocked segment, the affected settlements and the facilities whose catchment changes, each with its own caveats beside it.
  • The alert panel: a draft built only from confirmed claims, every sentence citing its source. Approving it records a name and states that nothing was sent.
  • The timeline: the evidence chain drawn on a horizontal axis — above the line what the community reported, below it what the system and reviewers did about it.

What the interface never does

  • It never shows a colour without an icon and a word. Every state is legible without relying on colour vision.
  • It never says "safe". Being outside the analysis area is stated as unchecked, and every impact panel carries the line "Places outside this analysis are not necessarily safe".
  • It never hides a decision behind a score. Confirm, dismiss, merge and separate are all explicit acts by a named person.

09What it deliberately does not do

Stating these is part of the design, not a disclaimer bolted on. A tool that is honest about its edges is more useful than one that is confidently wrong at them.

It does notBecause
Predict hazards, runout, flood extent, casualties or damageThose are physical models needing data we do not have. It answers the service-access question instead, which is answerable from what communities send.
Broadcast anythingEvery alert is a draft a person approves. The application has no sending capability at all.
Merge reports automaticallyA similarity score is evidence for a reviewer, never a decision.
Guess a locationA report with no GPS, pin or gazetteer match is left unplaced and never distance-compared.
Model multiple simultaneous blockagesThe analysis assumes one segment is blocked at a time. Real landslides often bury a longer stretch; this is a known limitation, not an oversight.
Treat a paraphrase as a copyCopies are judged on identical media, near-identical wording, or the same device. A light rewrite escapes detection today.
Cover the whole countryThe dataset is the Araniko corridor. Outside it the app declines to analyse and says so.
Replace official systemsBIPAD is the official record. This is community evidence that a reviewer has checked.

Known weaknesses, stated plainly

  • The evaluation set is small and hand-written. Five scenarios designed by the team. The system has never met a genuinely messy real dataset where forwarding means a screenshot, a re-recording, or a translation chain.
  • One reviewer does not scale to five hundred reports. The human-in-the-loop design is right for correctness and is the throughput bottleneck under a mass event.
  • Voice transcription is imperfect. Claims are checked against the transcript, and the transcript can itself contain errors — the honest limit of verifying a citation against a model's own reading.
  • OSM coverage in rural Nepal is uneven. The analysis reports weak map links as caveats and lists already-disconnected settlements as data gaps rather than as impact.
  • Model availability on a free tier is not guaranteed. The app carries a fallback ladder and degrades to "awaiting a person" rather than losing a report, but the strongest models are not always reachable.

10Scaling

The important property is this: value does not scale with adoption. Nobody needs fifty villagers to download anything. One honest observation — one person saying the road is blocked near Larcha — is enough to run the whole engine and produce a complete access analysis. This is not a social network that needs critical mass; it is a machine that needs one report and a road graph.

Technical scaling

LayerHow it scales
ApplicationCloud Run scales horizontally on requests. Stateless by design; no session affinity, no local state that matters.
DataFirestore scales automatically. Records are append-only where it matters (audit, extractions), which keeps writes cheap and history intact.
MediaCloud Storage, no practical ceiling. Streamed through an authorising route rather than served publicly.
GeographyThe expensive part is the offline preparation, not the runtime. The graph is pre-built and shipped; analysis is ~11 ms regardless of traffic.
Model costThe dominant per-report cost, and it is bounded: one extraction plus one embedding per report, plus one comparison per candidate pair that survives filtering.

Adding a region

Everything geographic is configuration, not code:

  1. Run scripts/prepare-region.ts against an Overpass query for the new area. It produces the road graph, assets and gazetteer as versioned JSON.
  2. Add a region config in src/config/regions/ — its name, time zone, hazard list with local names, and dataset reference.
  3. The app picks it up. Matching, impact analysis, the gazetteer and every screen read from the config.

A Philippines configuration already exists in the codebase, deliberately marked "no geographic dataset loaded, not validated" — the region switch works, the geography does not. It is a placeholder that demonstrates the seam rather than a claim about that country.

What would genuinely need to change at national scale

  • The reviewer bottleneck. Human confirmation is the point, but it must become a shift-based queue with assignment, not one person reading everything. Priority ordering by independent-source count and impact severity is the natural next step.
  • Multi-segment blockages. Real events block long stretches and cut several roads at once. The engine would need to accept a set of blocked segments rather than one.
  • A country-scale road graph. The current dataset is a corridor shipped in the image. Nationwide would mean a spatial store rather than a bundled JSON file.
  • Institutional integration. The natural end state is feeding confirmed incidents into BIPAD and police/army channels, in their format, with the provenance attached.
  • Offline-first intake. SMS, IVR and a store-and-forward mobile client for areas with no data coverage during an event.

Beyond Nepal

The mechanism generalises to any road-constrained, hazard-exposed region with usable map data: hill districts in India, landslide-prone areas of the Philippines, mountain corridors in Peru or Georgia. What changes is the dataset and the hazard list. What does not change is the chain — community evidence, incident intelligence, road-access impact, with provenance throughout — and that is the part worth carrying across borders.

11Future enhancements

Enhancement Effort Value
Report back to the reporter Small The receipt page today says what the system understood. It could also say what happened next — your report was confirmed, the road is being cleared. This closes the loop that makes someone report again.
Multiple blocked segments Medium Removes the most significant modelling limitation. The graph engine already handles segment removal; it needs to accept a set.
Priority queue for reviewers Medium Orders work by independent sources and computed impact rather than arrival time. Directly addresses the scaling bottleneck.
Paraphrase-resistant copy detection Medium Current detection catches identical wording. Embedding-similarity thresholds with human confirmation would catch the translation-chain and light-rewrite cases that matter in practice.
Offline and low-bandwidth intake Large SMS and IVR channels, plus a store-and-forward client. During a real event, coverage is exactly what fails.
BIPAD integration Large Pushes confirmed incidents into the official record rather than sitting beside it. Requires institutional agreement more than code.
Historical calibration Large Replay past monsoon seasons against recorded events to measure real recall and precision, replacing the hand-written evaluation set with something that can fail honestly.
Seasonal and weather context Medium Rainfall and soil-moisture context to rank which reported slips are most likely to worsen — stated as context, never as prediction.
The one we would do first. Reporting back to the reporter. It is the smallest change on this list and the only one that affects whether anyone uses the system a second time. Every other enhancement makes the machine better at its job; this one makes it worth someone's effort to feed.
